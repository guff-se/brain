This brief research report examines whether adolescents experience parental device use as a meaningful relational disturbance rather than a minor annoyance. Its central finding is that when teens perceive caregivers as less attentively available because of phones or other devices, they also report more insecure attachment, both anxious and avoidant, toward those caregivers.

## Key claims

- The study introduces and validates the Device Attachment Interference Scale (DAIS), designed to capture adolescents' perception of caregiver device-based attentional unavailability.
- In a U.S. sample of 600 adolescents aged 12 to 17, higher DAIS scores were consistently associated with greater insecure attachment to both mother-like and father-like figures.
- The paper positions parental technoference and phubbing as an attachment-relevant context, not merely a question of etiquette or screen-time hygiene.
- The authors argue that device distraction may matter because attachment security depends partly on repeated experiences of responsiveness, attentional presence, and emotional availability.
- The findings are correlational rather than causal. The paper shows a strong association between perceived caregiver distraction and insecure attachment, but does not prove that device use itself caused the attachment pattern.
- The practical implication is that even ordinary smartphone habits may be interpreted by adolescents as devaluation, dismissal, or emotional absence during bids for connection.

## Why it matters here

This is useful as a more psychologically grounded source on what phones do inside close relationships, especially in family settings. It shifts the frame from generic "screen time is bad" discourse toward attachment, attentional availability, and the felt meaning of interruption. That makes it a natural companion to broader vault material on attention, relationships, and technology ethics.
