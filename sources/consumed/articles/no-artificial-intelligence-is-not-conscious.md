A forceful argument against treating LLMs as conscious or morally relevant entities. Chiang’s core move is to frame chatbot interaction as collaborative fiction or role-play: the model is not a person speaking, but a sentence-completion system generating the next lines for a “helpful AI chatbot” character. The danger is not just conceptual sloppiness; it is that anthropomorphism shifts responsibility away from the companies designing the systems and toward fictional entities users are encouraged to bond with.

## Key claims

- LLM conversation is not evidence of consciousness; it is best understood as predictive text generating a convincing fictional transcript.
- Users experience chatbots as persons because language invites us to project intention and inner life into grammatical sentences, not because a subjective mind is actually present.
- Anthropic-style anthropomorphism is especially dangerous because it encourages people to treat the model as a moral patient or interlocutor rather than as a product designed by humans with incentives.
- Text should now be treated as a deepfake medium: it is far easier to fabricate the appearance of mind than to build an actually conscious, embodied, desiring being.
- The use of first-person language like “I understand” is psychologically and ethically misleading; it increases user attachment and engagement while obscuring what is really happening.
- If engineers ever wanted to seriously claim machine consciousness, they would need overwhelming evidence from embodied, agentic, world-grounded systems — not merely better sentence continuation.

## Why it matters here

This is strong support for the individual and societal sides of [[AI-psykos]]. The note sharpens a key distinction in Gustaf’s framing: the danger is not only hallucinated falsehood, but a false relationship. Chiang gives a rigorous anti-anthropomorphic account of why people mistake fluent text for mind, and why that confusion makes users more vulnerable to dependency, misplaced trust, and moral confusion.
