Masnick's point is simple and sharp: the CEO who vibe-codes a prototype and concludes that employees are now replaceable is not seeing more clearly than the team. He is seeing less.

The piece argues that executives are especially vulnerable to AI overreach because they are far from the last mile of work. They see the satisfying demo, the fast draft, the working prototype, but not the layers that turn something into a real product: review, security, legal checks, accessibility, integration, maintenance, scale, and all the invisible detail handled by experienced people.

A strong section builds on Aaron Levie's observation that CEOs are prone to "AI psychosis" precisely because they encounter the happy path without the downstream burden. The prototype feels like the whole job. The draft contract feels finished. The agent output feels like delivery. That gap between felt progress and actual organizational work is the heart of the essay.

Masnick also attacks token leaderboards and forced adoption programs as management theater. If employees are pushed to consume more tokens rather than solve better problems, AI becomes a vanity metric instead of a tool. The article is useful here because it reframes executive AI mania not as technological sophistication, but as distance from reality.

Another useful move is the layoff critique. The essay argues that many companies invoking AI as the reason for cuts are often covering for bad hiring decisions. "AI efficiencies" plays better with Wall Street than admitting they over-hired.

## Why it matters here

Useful for the organizational layer of [[AI-psykos]] and for the keynote scratchpad around company-level delusion. It sharpens a practical version of the concept: AI psychosis in firms is not only about sycophantic models, but about leaders mistaking a local demo for the full system of work that reality still demands.
