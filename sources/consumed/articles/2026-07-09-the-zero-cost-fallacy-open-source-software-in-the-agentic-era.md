Thoughtworks frames modern open source as a system under compound stress rather than a benign commons. The article's central move is to separate zero-cost distribution from expensive human maintenance, then argue that agentic AI intensifies the problem by flooding maintainers with low-quality contributions, degrading trust signals, and making "just re-implement it locally" sound newly plausible to enterprises.

## Key claims

- The "zero-cost fallacy" is the mistake of treating digital software's near-zero distribution cost as if it also erased the ongoing cost of human maintenance, stewardship, and emotional labor.
- Permissive licenses such as MIT and Apache helped software spread, but they also made it easy for large companies to capture value from volunteer-maintained infrastructure while returning little material support.
- Generative AI worsens the maintainer crisis by industrializing low-quality pull requests, making unpaid review work heavier and pushing some projects to close themselves off from public contribution.
- Traditional trust signals are weakening. Viral AI-agent projects can accumulate stars faster than they accumulate maturity, and malicious or careless contributions are cheaper to generate than before.
- Defensive licensing is not presented as a clean fix. Non-commercial clauses, dual licensing, and similar restrictions can trigger procurement avoidance, boycotts, or enforcement burdens that hurt adoption and exhaust maintainers in different ways.
- The article suggests a possible shift from shared code toward shared specifications plus local AI-assisted reimplementation, while warning that this only works reliably when specs and tests are unusually strong and may deepen inequality between well-resourced teams and everyone else.
- The practical prescription is institutional, not romantic: treat dependencies as load-bearing infrastructure, audit supply chains aggressively, and budget real money for patronage and contribution.
