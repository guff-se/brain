Seed for the AI-psychosis talk. Gustaf's framing, in his words:

> "My thoughts here is to bring it into my talk about AI psychosis, as an example of collective psychosis, a reference to the societal and organizational AI psychosis we're going through."

The move: use the [[Dancing plague of 1518 - Wikipedia]] as a historical anchor for the *collective* register of [[AI-psykos]] — not the individual user trapped in a sycophantic loop, but a whole population caught in the same compulsive behaviour at once. Strasbourg 1518 is the leading-theory case of stress-induced mass psychogenic illness: under famine and disease, one person starts, others mimic, and the behaviour spreads epidemically until it burns out. John Waller's phrase is "stress-induced psychosis" at population level.

What makes it useful as analogy, not just illustration:
- It is social contagion under stress, not individual pathology. That matches the Samhälle and Företag levels of AI-psykos better than the individual level does.
- The authorities made it worse by accommodating it — guild halls, musicians, space to dance — the institutional response amplified the contagion. Direct parallel to organizations that respond to AI-hype by building more dashboards, leaderboards, and token budgets (see [[Your CEO is suffering from AI psychosis]], the Uber token-leaderboard case, and Jensen Huang treating low token use as a red flag).
- It eventually stopped. Worth asking in the talk: what is the equivalent of the outbreak subsiding, and what is the equivalent of the mountaintop chapel.

Limits to flag honestly (so the analogy doesn't oversell): the dancing plague was acute, local, and visibly embodied; AI psychosis as Gustaf frames it is chronic, distributed, and invisible — closer to a slow erosion of the habit of being disagreed with than to a sudden compulsive break. The analogy earns its keep at the level of *mechanism* (stress + mimicry + institutional amplification), not symptom.
